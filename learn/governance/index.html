⚖️Governance and compliance in Azure

Microsoft Purview for data governance, Azure Policy for the rules of the estate, resource locks for the padlock, the Service Trust Portal for audit evidence — and the famous mix-up with RBAC, budgets, and retired Blueprints.

17 min read

  • ✦How Azure Policy enforces standards
  • ✦CanNotDelete vs ReadOnly locks
  • ✦How to separate Policy, RBAC, locks, budgets, and Blueprints on a question

⚖️Governance is how a company keeps Azure from turning into a junk drawer. You need to know what data you have, you need rules for how resources are built, and you need a padlock on the things that must not vanish. Three services. Three jobs. The exam will try to swap their name badges.

A useful picture: Purview is the librarian who knows which books exist and whether they are confidential. Azure Policy is the building code (“no wooden wiring, only these regions”). Resource locks are the glass case over the rare book — even the librarian cannot yank it out without unlocking it first.

1. Microsoft Purview

Microsoft Purview is Microsoft's data governance family. At AZ-900 level, remember the purpose, not every blade in the portal. Purview helps you discover, catalog, classify, and understand data across Azure and beyond — a data map and catalog, lineage, and compliance visibility so you can answer “where is our customer data, and who may use it?” It is not the tool that assigns the Contributor role, and it is not the tool that blocks a VM SKU.

Think

Purview = data librarian + catalog + compliance visibility. If the scenario is about sensitive data, catalogs, or “what data do we even have?”, think Purview.

Exam trap

Purview is not Azure Policy. Policy governs how Azure resources are configured. Purview governs data: find it, classify it, understand it. If the question never mentions data, Purview is probably the wrong answer.

2. Azure Policy

Azure Policy enforces rules on resources. Allowed locations. Allowed VM SKUs. Require a tag. Deny public IP addresses on NICs. You assign a policy (or a group of them, called an initiative) at a management group, subscription, or resource group, and it inherits down. Effects you will hear about include Audit (flag it), Deny (block the create or update), and deploy-if-not-exists (put a setting in place). Policy does not log people in and it does not grant Owner.

Think

Policy = the rulebook for resources. RBAC = who is allowed to try. A person can have permission (RBAC) and still be denied by Policy.

3. Resource locks

A resource lock is a padlock you put on a subscription, resource group, or resource. There are two types. CanNotDelete means you can still read and modify, but you cannot delete. ReadOnly means you cannot delete and you cannot change — you can only read. Even an Owner has to remove the lock before they can break the protection. That is the point: slow down accidents.

CanNotDelete

The usual production padlock

  • • Read: yes
  • • Change settings: yes
  • • Delete: no, until the lock is removed

ReadOnly

Hands off

  • • Read: yes
  • • Change settings: no
  • • Delete: no

Exam trap

A lock is not a backup. It does not copy your data. It does not replace RBAC — people still need roles to do their jobs. And a tag named DoNotDelete is just a sticky note. Only a lock is a lock.

4. The Service Trust Portal

The Microsoft Service Trust Portal (servicetrust.microsoft.com) is where Microsoft publishes the evidence of how its cloud services meet security, privacy, and compliance standards: independent audit reports (ISO 27001, SOC 1/2/3, PCI DSS, FedRAMP), whitepapers, and compliance guides. You sign in with a Microsoft cloud account to download most documents. It is a library of proof for auditors — not a tool that configures or checks your own resources.

Think

Auditor asks “show me Microsoft's SOC 2 report” → Service Trust Portal. Want to check YOUR resources against a standard → Azure Policy / Defender for Cloud regulatory compliance.

5. The mix-up table: keep these five apart

This is the highest-value page in the lesson. Microsoft will offer two of these as distractors on almost every governance question. Azure Blueprints used to package templates, Policy, and RBAC together. Blueprints are retired / legacy. Do not study them as a current feature. If they appear, they are usually a nostalgic wrong answer — not the star of today's exam.

AZ-900 exam points

  • ★Purview = data governance, catalog, compliance visibility
  • ★Azure Policy = resource rules that audit or deny
  • ★Locks = CanNotDelete or ReadOnly, even against Owners
  • ★RBAC is who; Policy is what; locks are the padlock; budgets are money
  • ★Blueprints are not the current thing to memorize

One sentence to remember

Purview governs data, Azure Policy governs how resources must look, resource locks padlock delete or change, RBAC still decides who may try, budgets watch money, and Blueprints are a legacy name you should not treat as today's answer.

Exam-style scene · Exam-style scene

Leadership wants every new resource blocked unless it is in Canada Central or Canada East, and they also want the production Recovery Services vault protected so even Owners cannot delete it on a Friday night.

Pick: Azure Policy for the allowed locations; a CanNotDelete lock on the vault.

Two different problems. Policy is the rule for the estate. The lock is the padlock on one precious resource. RBAC alone cannot express “no East US” or “Owners still cannot delete.”
